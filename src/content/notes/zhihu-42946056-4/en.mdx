> I have been reading *14 Lectures on Visual SLAM* and use this note to record key points.

This chapter introduces Lie groups and Lie algebras.

**Why use Lie groups and Lie algebras?** They simplify optimization problems involving pose variables. Using rotation and transformation matrices makes optimization constrained because a rotation matrix must remain orthogonal. Expressing rigid-body motion with Lie groups and Lie algebras turns it into an unconstrained optimization problem. This is their main use in visual SLAM.

## Definition of a group

**A group is an algebraic structure consisting of a set and an operation.** Let the set be $A$ and the operation be $\cdot$. It satisfies:

1. **Closure:** $\forall a_1,a_2\in A,\ a_1\cdot a_2\in A$.
2. **Associativity:** $\forall a_1,a_2,a_3\in A,\ (a_1\cdot a_2)\cdot a_3=a_1\cdot(a_2\cdot a_3)$.
3. **Identity:** $\exists a_0\in A$ such that $\forall a\in A,\ a_0\cdot a=a\cdot a_0=a$.
4. **Inverse:** $\forall a\in A,\ \exists a^{-1}\in A$ such that $a\cdot a^{-1}=a_0$.

**Common groups:**

1. General linear group $\mathrm{GL}(n)$: invertible $n\times n$ matrices under multiplication.
2. Special orthogonal group $\mathrm{SO}(n)$: rotation matrices; $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ and $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ are common.
3. Special Euclidean group $\mathrm{SE}(n)$: $n$-dimensional Euclidean transformations, including $\mathrm{SE}(2)$ and $\mathrm{SE}(3)$.

**A Lie group is a group with continuous, smooth structure.**

## Definition of a Lie algebra

Every Lie group has a corresponding Lie algebra that describes its local properties.

A Lie algebra consists of vector space $\mathbb V$, scalar field $\mathbb F$, and a binary operation (the Lie bracket) $[,]$. The triple $(\mathbb V,\mathbb F,[,])$ is a Lie algebra if it satisfies:

1. **Closure:** $\forall X,Y\in\mathbb V,\ [X,Y]\in\mathbb V$.
2. **Bilinearity:** for $\forall X,Y,Z\in\mathbb V$ and $a,b\in\mathbb F$, $[aX+bY,Z]=a[X,Z]+b[Y,Z]$ and $[Z,aX+bY]=a[Z,X]+b[Z,Y]$.
3. **Alternating property:** $\forall X\in\mathbb V,\ [X,X]=0$.
4. **Jacobi identity:** $\forall X,Y,Z\in\mathbb V$, $[X,[Y,Z]]+[Z,[X,Y]]+[Y,[Z,X]]=0$.

## Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3)$

$$
\mathfrak{so}(3)=
\left\{\phi\in\mathbb R^3,\
\Phi=\phi^\wedge\in\mathbb R^{3\times3}\right\}.
$$

1. Its elements are 3D vectors or $3\times3$ skew-symmetric matrices.
2. Its Lie bracket is $[\phi_1,\phi_2]=(\Phi_1\Phi_2-\Phi_2\Phi_1)^\vee$.
3. Its relation to $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ is $\mathbf R=\exp(\phi^\wedge)$.

## Lie algebra $\mathfrak{se}(3)$

$$
\mathfrak{se}(3)=
\left\{
\xi=\begin{bmatrix}\rho\\\phi\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb R^6,
\rho\in\mathbb R^3,\phi\in\mathfrak{so}(3),
\xi^\wedge=
\begin{bmatrix}\phi^\wedge&\rho\\\mathbf0^\mathsf T&0\end{bmatrix}
\in\mathbb R^{4\times4}
\right\}.
$$

1. Its elements are six-dimensional vectors: the first three components relate to translation and the last three to rotation.
2. Its Lie bracket is $[\xi_1,\xi_2]=(\xi_1^\wedge\xi_2^\wedge-\xi_2^\wedge\xi_1^\wedge)^\vee$.
3. Its relation to $\mathrm{SE}(3)$ is $\mathbf T=\exp(\xi^\wedge)$.

## Lie-algebra derivatives and perturbation models

For general matrix exponentials, the following is not true:

$$
\ln(\exp(\mathbf A)\exp(\mathbf B))=\mathbf A+\mathbf B.
$$

The **Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff (BCH)** formula gives the approximation

$$
\ln(\exp(\phi_1^\wedge)\exp(\phi_2^\wedge))^\vee
\approx
\begin{cases}
\mathbf J_l(\phi_2)^{-1}\phi_1+\phi_2,&\text{when }\phi_1\text{ is small},\\
\mathbf J_r(\phi_1)^{-1}\phi_2+\phi_1,&\text{when }\phi_2\text{ is small}.
\end{cases}
$$

This supports differentiation of rigid-body motion from the definition of a derivative:

$$
\frac{\partial\mathbf R\mathbf p}{\partial\varphi}
=\lim_{\varphi\to0}
\frac{\exp(\varphi^\wedge)\exp(\phi^\wedge)\mathbf p-\exp(\phi^\wedge)\mathbf p}{\varphi}
=-(\mathbf R\mathbf p)^\wedge.
$$

$$
\frac{\partial\mathbf T\mathbf p}{\partial\delta\xi}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf I&-(\mathbf R\mathbf p+\mathbf t)^\wedge\\
\mathbf0^\mathsf T&\mathbf0^\mathsf T
\end{bmatrix}
=(\mathbf T\mathbf p)^\odot.
$$

## Visual-SLAM application

Suppose we solve a motion-only bundle-adjustment problem:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\xi^*&=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\xi}\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf e\rVert^2,\\
\mathbf e&=\mathbf u_i-\frac{1}{s_i}\mathbf K\exp(\xi^\wedge)\mathbf p_i,\\
\mathbf T&=\exp(\xi^\wedge).
\end{aligned}
$$

With Gauss–Newton, take the linear term of the Taylor expansion of the residual:

$$
\mathbf e(\xi\oplus\delta\xi)=\mathbf e(\xi)+\mathbf J\delta\xi,
\qquad
\mathbf J=\frac{\partial\mathbf e}{\partial\delta\xi}.
$$

The key is computing Jacobian $\mathbf J$. Since $\mathbf u_i$ is a $2\times1$ vector independent of the derivative, introduce the map point in camera coordinates, $\mathbf P'$:

$$
\mathbf e=\mathbf u_i-\frac{1}{s_i}\mathbf K\mathbf P',
\qquad
\mathbf P'=\exp(\xi^\wedge)\mathbf p_i=\mathbf T\mathbf p_i.
$$

By the chain rule,

$$
\mathbf J=
\frac{\partial\mathbf e}{\partial\delta\xi}
=
\frac{\partial\mathbf e}{\partial\mathbf P'}
\frac{\partial\mathbf P'}{\partial\delta\xi}.
$$

The first factor is the derivative of the projection model:

$$
\frac{\partial\mathbf e}{\partial\mathbf P'}
=
-\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{f_x}{Z'}&0&-\frac{f_xX'}{Z'^2}\\
0&\frac{f_y}{Z'}&-\frac{f_yY'}{Z'^2}
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The second factor comes from the Lie-algebra perturbation model:

$$
\frac{\partial\mathbf P'}{\partial\delta\xi}
=
\frac{\partial\mathbf T\mathbf p_i}{\partial\delta\xi}
=
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf I&-\mathbf P'^\wedge\end{bmatrix}
=(\mathbf T\mathbf p_i)^\odot.
$$

This is the practical use of Lie algebra: it enables unconstrained optimization.
